# What OBS Studio Is

OBS Studio is software for making a video
feed out of pieces — a screen, a camera,
some words on the screen, a microphone —
and sending that feed to a recording, to
a live stream, or to both at once. It is
free and open source, maintained by the
OBS Project and a community of
contributors, and it runs on Windows,
macOS and Linux. If you have watched a
live stream, a recorded lesson or a video
podcast, you have very likely watched
something that passed through it.

The idea underneath is borrowed from
television. A TV studio has cameras,
microphones, a graphics machine and a
desk where someone chooses what the
audience sees, moment by moment. OBS puts
that desk on an ordinary computer. You
arrange your material into **scenes**
ahead of time — one scene might be a
title card, another your slides with your
camera in the corner — and while
recording or streaming you switch between
the scenes. What the audience gets is
the scene you have chosen, mixed live.

## What people use it for

- **Recording things that happen on a
  screen, with a person attached:** lessons,
  tutorials, software demonstrations,
  talks given from slides.
- **Recording and streaming meetings and
  events** where one composed feed — the
  speaker and the slides together — is
  worth more than a raw camera file.
- **Podcasts with video,** where two
  microphones and a camera or two become
  one finished programme.
- **Live streaming** to services such as
  Twitch and YouTube, which is the job it
  was first built for.
- **Feeding other applications** through
  its virtual camera: OBS composes the
  picture, and your meeting software
  receives it as if it were a webcam.

## What it is not

- **Not a video editor.** There is no
  timeline. OBS records the performance
  as it happens; trimming, cutting and
  titles-after-the-fact happen in an
  editor afterwards. Chapter 14 returns
  to this.
- **Not a meeting service.** It hosts
  nothing and nobody joins it. It can
  *feed* a meeting (virtual camera) or
  *record* one (by capturing your
  screen), but the meeting itself lives
  elsewhere.
- **Not a hosted platform.** Nothing is
  uploaded anywhere unless you press
  Start Streaming. A recording is a file
  on your own disk.
- **Not automatic.** Somebody composes
  the scenes and presses the buttons.
  That somebody is the subject of this
  guide.

## The one-sentence version

If a camera pointed at a screen is not
enough — if you want the slides *and* the
speaker, the title card *and* the break
notice, in one file or one stream — OBS
is the desk where you mix those pieces
live, at the price of learning how the
desk is laid out.
